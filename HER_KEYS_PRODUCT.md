# Her Keys — Product Contract

## 1. Product Definition

Her Keys is an AI-powered Life Operating System for mothers rebuilding their lives and household infrastructure after separation or divorce.

Separation frequently breaks the informal operating system that previously coordinated:

- children
- calendars
- household responsibilities
- finances
- meals
- work
- appointments
- co-parenting logistics
- recurring responsibilities
- decisions
- planning
- mental load

Her Keys helps rebuild that infrastructure around the user.

The product should reduce the amount of information, coordination, decision-making, and responsibility the user must manually hold in her head.

Her Keys is NOT primarily:

- a to-do list
- a calendar
- a budgeting app
- a meal planner
- a co-parenting messenger
- a divorce app
- a productivity tracker
- an AI chatbot

Those may exist as capabilities inside a larger intelligent operating system.

## 2. Brand Promise

Rebuild your life. Run it your way.

## 3. Core Product Principle

Do not simply help the user manage more.

Help her have less to manage.

Her Keys should help the user:

- identify what matters
- determine what can wait
- eliminate unnecessary decisions
- detect overloaded days
- identify scheduling risk
- recognize recurring behavioral patterns
- uncover underlying problems
- break overwhelming problems into tiny actions
- delegate responsibilities
- automate repeatable work
- create sustainable household systems
- reduce mental load

Success should NOT primarily be measured by tasks created or tasks completed.

Important success measures include:

- time recovered
- decisions eliminated
- tasks delegated
- tasks automated
- conflicts prevented
- obligations intentionally dropped
- recurring problems eliminated
- household systems established
- reduction in overload

# 4. Intelligence Architecture

Her Keys should eventually operate through several cooperating intelligence systems.

They should share household context rather than behave like disconnected AI features.

## Daily Load Agent

Core question:

"What is likely to go wrong today?"

Inputs may include:

- calendar
- to-do list
- work commitments
- children
- school events
- custody schedule
- household responsibilities
- meals
- appointments
- travel time
- transition time
- deadlines
- task duration
- task flexibility
- dependencies

Responsibilities:

- estimate realistic available capacity
- compare tasks against calendar availability
- identify unrealistic daily plans
- detect scheduling conflicts
- identify insufficient transition time
- identify task overload
- identify cascade risk
- determine which commitments are movable
- recommend a SPECIFIC item to move, drop, delegate, shorten, or replace

The Daily Load Agent must not merely say:

"You have a busy day."

It should make a concrete recommendation such as:

"Your day has only 20 minutes of unscheduled buffer. Returning the Target items is flexible and not due today. Moving it to tomorrow gives you a 65-minute buffer before soccer practice. I recommend moving it."

The user should be able to:

- Accept
- Keep current plan
- Ask for another solution

High-consequence commitments must never be silently moved.

# 5. Momentum Agent

Core question:

"Where does the user repeatedly get stuck?"

The Momentum Agent analyzes longitudinal behavior rather than only today's schedule.

Potential patterns include:

- chronic rescheduling
- repeated avoidance
- unrealistic time estimates
- oversized tasks
- overplanning
- recurring interruptions
- poor task timing
- dependency bottlenecks
- excessive context switching
- consistently overloaded days
- tasks that repeatedly fail during particular times
- responsibilities that should potentially be delegated

The system must distinguish:

OBSERVATION
from
HYPOTHESIS
from
CONCLUSION.

Example:

Observation:

"You have moved 'Review monthly expenses' six times."

Additional evidence:

"Five of those attempts were scheduled after 7 PM. You complete relatively few administrative tasks during that time."

Hypothesis:

"Evening may not be a good time for financial administration."

The system should test this rather than treating it as fact.

Do not diagnose ADHD, anxiety disorders, depression, or other medical/psychological conditions.

---

# 6. Discovery Agent — Talk It Out

A user should NOT need to know exactly what her problem is before Her Keys can help.

The application must allow the user to speak naturally to Her Keys.

Examples:

"I don't know what's wrong. I'm just overwhelmed."

"I feel like I'm doing things all day but nothing gets finished."

"Something about Sundays makes me miserable."

"I can't keep up with the house."

"I keep putting this off and I don't know why."

"I feel like I'm always behind."

The Discovery Agent should investigate before giving advice.

Core discovery loop:

LISTEN
→ EXTRACT
→ GENERATE COMPETING HYPOTHESES
→ CLARIFY
→ CHECK AVAILABLE EVIDENCE
→ TEST
→ RECOMMEND

Potential extracted information includes:

- people
- responsibilities
- time constraints
- recurring events
- unfinished obligations
- decisions
- dependencies
- areas of pressure
- repeated language
- possible bottlenecks

The agent should ask ONE useful question at a time.

It should attempt to distinguish symptoms from underlying system problems.

Example:

User says:

"My house is always messy."

The agent should not automatically recommend a cleaning schedule.

It may discover that:

- children have no landing area for school/sports items
- belongings accumulate in common areas
- the user repeatedly reorganizes them
- mornings become chaotic

The actual system problem may therefore be the absence of a household landing system rather than insufficient cleaning.

The agent must be allowed to say:

"I don't know yet."

Uncertainty is preferable to fabricated certainty.

---

# 7. One Move

One Move is a central Her Keys interaction.

Core question:

"What is the smallest realistic action the user can take today that creates meaningful forward movement?"

The system should reduce friction rather than generate another large task.

Examples:

DO NOT:

"Create a budget."

Instead:

"Open your checking account and find your current balance."

DO NOT:

"Update your resume."

Instead:

"Open your resume and update your current job title."

DO NOT:

"Clean the garage."

Instead:

"Put one empty donation box by the garage door."

DO NOT:

"Organize all the kids' school supplies."

Instead:

"Put one basket near the door for backpacks."

One Move should create momentum rather than guilt.

If the Daily Load Agent determines the user's day is already overloaded, the correct recommendation may be:

"No additional One Move today."

The system must understand that NOT adding another obligation can be the correct intervention.

# 9. Mental Load / Stress & Avoidance Matrix

The application should establish a baseline across major life domains.

Do NOT present an internally invented metric as a clinical anxiety diagnosis.

For each domain consider capturing:

- Pressure
- Confidence
- Avoidance
- Frequency

Domains may include:

- Money
- Kids
- Home
- Career
- Meals
- Co-parenting
- Calendar
- Personal responsibilities

Example:

Money:
Pressure: 5
Avoidance: 5
Confidence: 2

Kids:
Pressure: 4
Avoidance: 1
Confidence: 5

This distinction matters.

Something can create significant pressure while still being managed effectively.

The intelligence system should identify where pressure combines with avoidance, low confidence, or repeated behavioral problems.

# 14. Evidence and Learning

Keep stated user beliefs separate from observed behavioral evidence.

Example:

STATED BELIEF:

"I'm terrible with money."

OBSERVED BEHAVIOR:

"96% of financial obligations were completed on time."

Her Keys should eventually be capable of recognizing:

"The evidence doesn't suggest that you're unreliable with money. Financial decisions appear to create significant pressure for you, which is different."

The system must be capable of updating its hypotheses as evidence accumulates.

Do not permanently label the user based on onboarding answers.

---

# 15. AI Confidence

AI-generated interpretations should have an internal confidence model.

Conceptually:

POSSIBLE PATTERN
Limited evidence.

LIKELY PATTERN
Conversation and behavioral evidence support the hypothesis.

ESTABLISHED PATTERN
Repeated longitudinal evidence supports the pattern.

User-facing language should reflect confidence.

Examples:

Low confidence:
"I have a theory."

Higher confidence:
"I've seen this pattern repeatedly over the last six weeks."

Never fabricate certainty.

---

# 16. Autonomy and Safety

Her Keys should eventually support three conceptual autonomy levels:

1. Recommend
2. Approve & Execute
3. Autopilot

High-consequence actions always require explicit approval.

Examples include:

- custody changes
- financial transactions
- medical actions
- important appointments
- children's commitments
- legal matters

Important recommendations should be explainable.

The user should be able to understand:

- what Her Keys noticed
- why it matters
- what Her Keys recommends
- what will happen if she approves

# 18. Design Philosophy

The experience should feel:

- calm
- intelligent
- supportive
- capable
- adult
- trustworthy
- premium

Avoid:

- clinical mental-health aesthetics
- divorce clichés
- excessive pink
- productivity-dashboard overload
- guilt-based UX
- childish illustrations
- excessive gamification
- overwhelming amounts of information

Primary UX principle:

ONE THING AT A TIME.

The desired emotional outcome is:

"I don't have to hold everything in my head anymore."

---

# 19. Build Philosophy

Do not build every feature simultaneously.

The first product experience should prove:

"Can Her Keys meaningfully reduce mental load?"

Early builds should prioritize:

1. Onboarding / Life Systems Audit
2. Today
3. Calendar
4. Tasks
5. Daily Load Agent experience
6. One Move
7. Talk It Out
8. Kids
9. Home
10. Money

Use seeded data before implementing complex integrations.

Do not let implementation convenience turn Her Keys into a generic task manager.

The differentiating product is the intelligence connecting the user's life systems.
